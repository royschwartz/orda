Art direction brief

Physical Face Project

The Context

Of the 24 units, 18 have faces; I’ll refer to those units as Faces. The Elders don’t have faces.

Each cube may carry a face on two sides: the front and the back. I’m still working this out, and we’ll need to see how it feels once the game is playable. When a unit interacts with another hex—for example, by moving toward it—the cube rotates in that direction and orients its faces toward the action. With the same face on the front and back, a face should always be visible from a suitable camera angle. The top and bottom of the cube display the unit symbol. The remaining sides are either blank or, in some cases, display symbolic images.

The player should propel the cube across the board with the mouse in a very tactile way. It bounces across the hexes—which are themselves 3D-scanned—almost in slow motion, striking the board with echoing thuds.

Vines grow up the sides of the cube to indicate the Face’s health, and they bristle as the cube moves.

The Faces

The plan is for the artist to provide five core expressions for each unit:

  • Default
  • Excited/angry
  • Hurt
  • Sleeping — eyes closed; this could potentially be done in post-production.
  • Dying — a devolution effect that could also be reversed and used for birth.
Special faces (there may be more)
  • Ram — lowers its face, showing the top of its head as it performs its special attack.
  • Spitter — puffs up and spits out a projectile.
  • Scorpion — opens its mouth and releases poison gas.
  • Moth — releases fog from its mouth.
  • Psychic — closes its eyes when entering a trance.
  • Mother — smiles beatifically.
  • Tree — opens its mouth extremely wide, and units and items fly out.
  • Siren — opens its mouth to sing.

If there are 18 Faces, each should ultimately have five core expressions, plus any special expressions it needs. Separate casts may be unnecessary, especially for something like sleep, where we may only need the eyelids to close. I’ve started getting good results with inexpensive Claytoon. At least, it looked good before I scanned it; there’s a real learning curve. Still, there may be something useful there.

Once the expressions are delivered, I’ll animate them separately in Blender.

Animation and Behavior

Faces change according to programmatic rules. A unit locked in combat will usually show its excited expression; when it takes a hit, the hurt expression may flash. We’ll animate seamless transitions between these expressions.

When a unit commits to a new type of action, it may need a special expression. For example, a unit using rockets could show the spitter expression. This may be preferable to creating a separate spitting animation for every Face. When concealed units use rockets, the spitting face will emerge; otherwise, concealed units will show no face.

Visual Direction

I’m not yet sure exactly what the faces should look like. Right now, I want them to feel ancient, remain readable from a distance, and have a certain simplicity. They should work well with shadows and may include realistic features—such as cheekbones—that catch the light and reveal different aspects of the face as the cube turns.

For color, we should stick to one or two main colors. Subtle details can become visible up close, as long as they don’t hurt readability from a distance. I’m still unsure about the default face color: flesh tones, or something more rock-like?

Artist Expectations

The first phase should be collaborative. We’ll work together—by call or text; I have no preference—to find the right material and workflow and establish the visual style for the faces. All faces should feel consistent in this respect. Once those decisions are made, the artist can iterate and complete the project with less involvement from me.
